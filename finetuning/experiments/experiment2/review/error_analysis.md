## What this means for Experiment 2 vs. Experiment 1

Experiment 1's dominant failure mode was picking index 0 almost regardless of content (a raw positional shortcut). None of these 14 Experiment 2 errors show that pattern in its crude form — the model is no longer just "guessing the first sentence." But **5 of the 14 (the "too vague" category) are a subtler residual of the same instinct**: the model still gravitates toward a generic, definitional-*sounding* sentence ("X is a type of Y," "X is the loss of Y," "X stands for Y") even when it isn't the first candidate and even when a more substantive explanation is available elsewhere in the list. The shuffle-based debiasing in Experiment 2 fixed the *positional* trigger for this but not the underlying *content preference* for generic-sounding definitions.

The other dominant cluster — **related fact instead of explanation (35.7%)** — is a genuinely different problem: the model is drawn to sentences that are topically on-target (cause, risk, severity, composition, benefit) but don't actually answer "what does this term mean," which is a direct violation of the system prompt's explicit rule 2 ("explain the term — not just something about the topic"). This wasn't a focus of Experiment 2's fixes at all.

## Proposed training data for Experiment 3 (based strictly on these observed patterns)

1. **Contrastive "generic-vs-functional" examples** (targets the 35.7% "too vague" cluster): add/re-weight training examples where a generic classification sentence ("X is a type/kind of Y") is present in the candidate list but is *not* the correct answer, paired with the correct answer being a sentence that explains function, mechanism, symptoms, or consequence. The goal is to teach that a definitional-sounding sentence is not automatically preferable just because it sounds like a definition.

2. **Contrastive "related-fact-vs-explanation" examples** (targets the 35.7% "related fact" cluster): add examples specifically containing plausible distractor sentences about *cause/etiology*, *severity/spectrum*, *future risk*, or *composition/properties* — i.e., sentences that are true and topically relevant but answer a different question than "what is this term." Several of today's errors (Hepatitis C's etiology sentence, AUD's severity sentence, High Blood Pressure in Pregnancy's future-risk sentence, Inhalants' properties sentence) are exactly this shape; more labeled examples with this specific kind of distractor should help the model learn the distinction the system prompt already states but the model isn't reliably applying.

3. **Compound/multi-part terms** (targets the 14.3% "too specific" cluster, 2 of 2 examples): both instances here involve compound terms ("Vision Impairment **and** Blindness," "Cleaning, Disinfecting, **and** Sanitizing") where the model latched onto only one component. Add more labeled examples with `X, Y, and Z`- or `X and Y`-style compound terms so the model learns to prefer sentences that address the term's full scope, not just a piece of it.

4. **Procedural/"how-to" terms** (targets the 7.1% "treatment/prevention" case): only one example here, but it's a distinct term *type* ("How to Lower Cholesterol with Diet") that may be underrepresented in the current 176-example pool. A handful of additional "how-to"/procedural-title examples, specifically labeled to reject numeric-guideline/advice sentences in favor of conceptual ones, would directly reinforce the system prompt's existing rule against recommendation-style answers for this term shape.

5. **FAQ/question-formatted source articles** (targets the 7.1% "question/header" case): this is a new, previously undocumented failure mode. Since MedlinePlus articles for broad/topic-style terms are often structured as a series of questions and answers, their candidate lists contain literal questions as candidate sentences. Add a small number of examples specifically drawn from such FAQ-structured articles, with the correct label always on a substantive answer sentence (never the question header), so the model has explicit signal that a bare question is never a valid pick. (Separately worth flagging, though outside the scope of "training data": since a question can never be a valid standalone explanation per the system prompt's own rules, it may also be worth revisiting whether question-form candidates should be filtered out at the candidate-generation stage — but that is a data/pipeline change, not a training-data addition, so it is noted here only for awareness.)

Given all 14 errors here come from a small, already-diagnosed set of content-judgment gaps rather than position or length artifacts, Experiment 3's likely highest-leverage move is **quality-focused hard-negative labeling** (items 1–2 above cover 71% of the observed errors) rather than another round of pure distribution/position correction.

Nothing was changed in any dataset, model, or evaluation as part of this analysis.
